Instantaneous Rate of Change

Velocity and Other Rates of Change · Based on Mr. Keith Penski’s class notes

Page 1 · Opening definition

What is an instantaneous rate of change?

Mr. Penski begins with the central idea:

The instantaneous rate of change of \(f(x)\) at \(a\) is the derivative.\[f'(a)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}\]

The limit exists when the derivative exists at that point. The derivative describes how quickly a function is changing at one particular input.

Think of a car’s speedometer

Average rate of change

You travel 120 miles in 2 hours. Your average speed is 60 mph.

\[\frac{120\text{ miles}}{2\text{ hours}}=60\text{ mph}\]
Instantaneous rate of change

You glance at your speedometer at one particular instant. It reads 72 mph.

The derivative is the mathematical equivalent of that instantaneous reading.

Key distinction: An average describes an interval; an instantaneous rate describes one moment.

Where does the derivative formula come from?

Choose two points on a curve:

\[(a,f(a))\qquad\text{and}\qquad(a+h,f(a+h))\]

The slope of the line joining them (the secant line) is rise divided by run:

\[\text{Average rate of change}=\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{(a+h)-a}=\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}\]

As \(h\) approaches zero, the second point approaches the first. The secant line approaches a tangent line. Its limiting slope is the instantaneous rate of change.

Why not simply substitute \(h=0\)? The original quotient would have a zero denominator. A limit asks what the quotient approaches, without dividing by zero.

Problem 2: How does a circle's area change with its circumference?

Reconstructed directions (not a verbatim transcription of the textbook): Given \(A=\pi r^2\) and \(C=2\pi r\), (a) express area as a function of circumference, (b) find \(\frac{dA}{dC}\), (c) evaluate the derivative at \(C=\pi\) and \(C=6\pi\), and (d) state its units if circumference is measured in inches.

(a) Express area in terms of circumference

We are measuring how area changes when circumference changes, so first make circumference the input variable. Solve for radius, then substitute:

\[r=\frac{C}{2\pi}\qquad A=\pi\left(\frac{C}{2\pi}\right)^2=\frac{C^2}{4\pi}\]

Result: \(A(C)=\frac{C^2}{4\pi}\). The notation \(A(C)\) emphasizes that area is now a function of circumference.

(b) Differentiate — one step at a time

Step 1. Write the constant separately. Remember that \(\pi\) is a number, not a variable.

\[A(C)=\frac{1}{4\pi}C^2\]

Step 2. Use the Constant Multiple Rule: keep \(\frac{1}{4\pi}\) and differentiate \(C^2\).

\[\frac{dA}{dC}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{d}{dC}(C^2)\]

Step 3. Use the Power Rule: bring down the exponent 2 and reduce the exponent by 1.

\[\frac{d}{dC}(C^2)=2C^{2-1}=2C\]

Step 4. Multiply, then simplify:

\[\frac{dA}{dC}=\frac{1}{4\pi}(2C)=\frac{2C}{4\pi}=\boxed{\frac{C}{2\pi}}\]
Meaning: \(\frac{dA}{dC}\) measures the instantaneous change in area per unit increase in circumference. Since \(\frac{C}{2\pi}=r\), the rate is numerically equal to the circle's radius.

(c) Evaluate at the specified circumferences

When \(C=\pi\)
\[\left.\frac{dA}{dC}\right|_{C=\pi}=\frac{\pi}{2\pi}=\boxed{\frac12}\]
When \(C=6\pi\)
\[\left.\frac{dA}{dC}\right|_{C=6\pi}=\frac{6\pi}{2\pi}=\boxed{3}\]

As circumference increases, area grows faster per additional unit of circumference.

(d) Identify the units

Area is in square inches and circumference in inches, so the units of its derivative are:

\[\frac{\text{in}^2}{\text{in}}=\boxed{\text{in}}\]

The notes show the ratio \(\text{in}^2/\text{in}\), which simplifies to inches.

Try it yourself

If the circumference is \(10\pi\) inches, what is \(\frac{dA}{dC}\)?

Review checklist

Before moving on, be able to explain why we rewrite area as a function of \(C\), how the Constant Multiple and Power Rules work together, why \(\frac{dA}{dC}=r\), and how to determine derivative units.

Motion Along a Line: Position, Velocity, and Speed

Suppose an object moves along a straight coordinate line. Its position at time t is \(s=f(t)\). Positive and negative positions tell where it is relative to the origin; they do not tell its direction of motion.

Displacement and average velocity

\[\Delta s=s(t_2)-s(t_1)\qquad v_{\text{avg}}=\frac{s(t_2)-s(t_1)}{t_2-t_1}\]

Displacement is final position minus initial position. Average velocity divides displacement by elapsed time. It is the slope of a secant line on a position–time graph.

Instantaneous velocity and speed

\[v(t)=s'(t)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{s(t+h)-s(t)}{h}\qquad \text{Speed}=|v(t)|\]
Velocity

Includes direction. Positive means motion toward increasing position; negative means motion toward decreasing position.

\[v=-40\text{ mph}\]
Speed

Magnitude only. It is never negative.

\[|v|=40\text{ mph}\]
Important: Negative velocity does not necessarily mean slowing down. It means moving in the negative direction. To determine speeding up or slowing down, we also need to consider acceleration.

Round trip: average speed versus average velocity

A student walks 30 feet to the right, then 30 feet back, in 20 seconds. The displacement is 0 feet, but the distance traveled is 60 feet.

\[\text{Average velocity}=\frac{0}{20}=0\text{ ft/s}\qquad\text{Average speed}=\frac{60}{20}=3\text{ ft/s}\]

Problem 8: Water draining from a tank

Directions reconstructed from the handwritten solution (not verified textbook wording): The amount of water remaining in a tank after t minutes is \(Q(t)=200(30-t)^2\) gallons. (a) Find the instantaneous rate of change at 10 minutes using the limit definition. (b) Find the average rate of change over the first 10 minutes.

What are we finding? \(Q(10)\) tells how much water is left; \(Q'(10)\) tells how fast the amount is changing at that instant. A negative rate means the quantity is decreasing.

Part (a): Limit definition — complete worked solution

Step 1 — Write the derivative definition at 10 minutes. We compare water at 10 minutes with water at a nearby time \(10+h\), divide by elapsed time \(h\), then let that interval approach zero.

\[Q'(10)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{Q(10+h)-Q(10)}{h}\]

Step 2 — Calculate the two quantities. Substitute 10 into the original function, then substitute the entire expression \(10+h\) for \(t\). The minus sign before the parentheses changes the signs of both terms.

\[Q(10)=200(30-10)^2=200(20)^2=80{,}000\]\[Q(10+h)=200\bigl(30-(10+h)\bigr)^2=200(20-h)^2\]

Step 3 — Substitute into the difference quotient. Do not put \(h=0\) yet: that would give \(0/0\).

\[Q'(10)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{200(20-h)^2-80{,}000}{h}\]

Step 4 — Expand and combine like terms. Use \((a-b)^2=a^2-2ab+b^2\), then distribute 200. The constant terms cancel.

\[(20-h)^2=400-40h+h^2\]\[\begin{aligned}Q'(10)&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{200(400-40h+h^2)-80{,}000}{h}\\&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{80{,}000-8{,}000h+200h^2-80{,}000}{h}\\&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{-8{,}000h+200h^2}{h}\end{aligned}\]

Step 5 — Factor and cancel \(h\). We may cancel because \(h\) is approaching zero, not equal to zero during the simplification.

\[\begin{aligned}Q'(10)&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{h(-8{,}000+200h)}{h}\\&=\lim_{h\to0}(-8{,}000+200h)\end{aligned}\]

Step 6 — Evaluate the limit. Now substitution is valid.

\[Q'(10)=-8{,}000+200(0)=\boxed{-8{,}000\ \text{gal/min}}\]
Interpretation: At exactly 10 minutes, the amount of water remaining is decreasing at an instantaneous rate of 8,000 gallons per minute. This is a rate, not the amount of water in the tank.

Part (b): Average rate over the first 10 minutes

This is a different interval from the moving secant above. Use the values at \(t=0\) and \(t=10\).

\[Q(0)=200(30)^2=180{,}000\]\[\frac{Q(10)-Q(0)}{10-0}=\frac{80{,}000-180{,}000}{10}=\boxed{-10{,}000\ \text{gal/min}}\]

The average rate during the first 10 minutes is −10,000 gal/min, whereas the instantaneous rate at 10 minutes is −8,000 gal/min. The water is still draining, but its rate of loss is becoming less negative.

Knowledge check

Why is it valid to cancel the factor \(h\) in Step 5?

Worked Example: Dynamite Launches a Rock

A dynamite blast sends a heavy rock straight upward at 160 ft/s (about 109 mph). Its height after \(t\) seconds is \(s(t)=160t-16t^2\), with height in feet and time in seconds. We ignore air resistance.

\[\underbrace{s(t)=160t-16t^2}_{\text{height (ft)}}\quad\xrightarrow{\text{derivative}}\quad\underbrace{v(t)=160-32t}_{\text{velocity (ft/s)}}\quad\xrightarrow{\text{derivative}}\quad\underbrace{a(t)=-32}_{\text{acceleration (ft/s}^2\text{)}}\]

Interactive flight graph: height versus time

Drag the time slider or choose a key moment. The moving point shows the rock's height; the tangent line shows its instantaneous velocity as a slope. The graph rises until 5 seconds, then falls.

0.0 s
Height: 0 ftVelocity: 160 ft/sSpeed: 160 ft/sAcceleration: −32 ft/s²

The rock launches upward with positive velocity.

Part (a): How high does the rock go?

What are we finding? The maximum value of the height function. At the top, the rock momentarily stops rising, so its velocity is zero.

\[v(t)=s'(t)=160-32t\]\[0=160-32t\implies t=5\text{ s}\]\[s(5)=160(5)-16(5)^2=800-400=\boxed{400\text{ ft}}\]

Interpretation: The rock reaches a maximum height of 400 feet at 5 seconds. The velocity changes from positive to negative there.

Part (b): Velocity and speed at 256 feet—up and down

What are we finding? Both times the rock is at 256 feet, then its velocity (signed) and speed (nonnegative) at each time.

Step 1 — Set the height to 256 and solve for time.

\[256=160t-16t^2\]\[0=16t^2-160t+256=16(t-2)(t-8)\]\[\boxed{t=2\text{ s}\quad\text{or}\quad t=8\text{ s}}\]

Step 2 — Substitute each time into velocity.

\[v(2)=160-32(2)=\boxed{+96\text{ ft/s}}\]\[v(8)=160-32(8)=\boxed{-96\text{ ft/s}}\]

Step 3 — Take absolute values to find speed.

\[\text{Speed}=|v(t)|\implies |+96|=|-96|=\boxed{96\text{ ft/s}}\]
At 2 seconds: rising

Height 256 ft; velocity +96 ft/s; speed 96 ft/s.

At 8 seconds: falling

Height 256 ft; velocity −96 ft/s; speed 96 ft/s.

The speeds match, but the velocity signs differ because the directions differ.

Part (c): What is the acceleration at any time?

What are we finding? How quickly velocity changes. Differentiate velocity.

\[a(t)=v'(t)=\frac{d}{dt}(160-32t)=\boxed{-32\text{ ft/s}^2}\]

Acceleration remains downward even at the highest point, where velocity is momentarily zero. On the way up, the rock slows down; on the way down, it speeds up.

Part (d): When does the rock hit the ground?

What are we finding? The time when height returns to zero, excluding the instant of launch.

\[0=160t-16t^2=16t(10-t)\]\[t=0\text{ (launch)}\quad\text{or}\quad\boxed{t=10\text{ s (landing)}}\]
